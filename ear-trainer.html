The short answer: Western music notation was invented before standardized tuning, and we've been stuck with it ever since.


The piano has 12 notes per octave. But music notation was built around 7 letter names (A B C D E F G). When you need notes between the letters, you use sharps (#) and flats (♭). The result: some notes have two names.


E# and F are the same key on the piano. So are B# and C, C♭ and B, F♭ and E. These are called enharmonic equivalents — same pitch, different name.


Why does E# exist at all? Because in certain keys (like F# major), every note in the scale needs its own letter. The 7th note is E#, not F — even though they sound identical — because you can't have two F's and zero E's in a scale.


Minor scales: A minor scale is like a major scale but with a darker mood. The key difference: the 3rd, 6th, and 7th notes are lowered by a half step. C major = C D E F G A B. C minor = C D E♭ F G A♭ B♭. That flatted 3rd is what gives minor its characteristic "sad" sound.
